# Ozon reference-image analysis

## Scope and evidence limit

The source directory contains 15 portrait images, mostly 3:4 listing-cover compositions rather than complete eight-image sets. They span home goods, kitchenware, tools, garden equipment, and accessories. They can reveal recurring attention and information-design patterns, but they cannot prove causation, set-wide style consistency, or conversion lift because no per-image traffic, experiment, price, review, category-rank, or competitor-control data was supplied.

Treat all conclusions below as design hypotheses. Preserve useful mechanisms; do not inherit unsupported claims or visual clutter.

## Cross-sample findings

### Likely click-supporting mechanisms

- Make the product or bundle recognizable within one glance; strong examples give it the largest visual mass.
- Use a large Russian category headline near the top, followed by one numeric anchor such as quantity, size, runtime, hardness, or bundle count.
- Create strong figure-ground contrast: dark tools against light panels, warm candlelight against muted neutrals, or bright product color against a restrained background.
- Show an emotionally or functionally legible context: pillows with a person or cat, candles on a cake, food on a grill, or a pruner cutting a branch.
- Use category-coded color without losing product fidelity: warm gold for candles, fresh outdoor greens for garden tools, neutral cream for homeware, and restrained high-contrast accents for technical products.

### Likely conversion-supporting mechanisms

- Reduce uncertainty with exact dimensions, quantity, capacity, package contents, material, compatibility, runtime, or power.
- Show the received set and the product in use rather than relying only on a decorative hero.
- Convert complex specifications into a visible hierarchy of headline, numeric proof, and 2–4 short benefits.
- Use real detail crops and scale cues to answer objections before purchase.
- Keep a real brand or official-store indicator only when the user supplies it and the claim is verifiable.

### Repeated weaknesses and risks

- Several covers carry too many badges and small captions to remain legible in search thumbnails.
- Numeric claims, country flags, technology seals, medals, and superlatives may look persuasive while being unverified or misleading.
- Some compositions resemble a website screenshot or a collage rather than a controlled marketplace asset.
- Decorative typefaces and mixed type systems reduce readability and weaken set consistency.
- Generative or aggressive compositing can distort product size, included quantity, attachments, edge shape, or material.
- A visually attractive reference may violate current category-specific main-image rules. Recheck Ozon requirements at runtime.
- High information density is not automatically high conversion; it can increase cognitive load and distrust.

## Design rules derived from the sample

1. Give the product 55–75% of the meaningful visual area on hero and clean product cards.
2. Use one headline, one numeric anchor, and at most two supporting facts on the hero.
3. Use 2–4 benefits on dedicated infographic slides, not eight micro-badges on the cover.
4. Reserve emotional/lifestyle imagery for scenes; keep a separate exact-product white-background view.
5. Use numbers only when they resolve a buyer question and have a fact-ledger source.
6. Use one tokenized visual system across the set while varying composition by slide purpose.
7. Treat badges, flags, medals, ratings, certifications, and comparison claims as prohibited unless the user provides explicit evidence and Ozon currently permits them.
